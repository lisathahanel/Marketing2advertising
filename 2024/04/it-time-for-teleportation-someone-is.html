Quantum States

In quantum mechanics, a quantum system is described by a mathematical construct called a quantum state. A quantum state represents all the information about a system, including the probabilities of different outcomes when a measurement is made. Quantum states are typically represented by wave functions or state vectors. The key features of quantum states are:

1. **Superposition**: 

A fundamental property of quantum states is superposition. This means that a quantum system can exist in a linear combination of multiple states simultaneously. For example, an electron's quantum state can be a superposition of "spin up" and "spin down" states until a measurement is made.

2. **Measurement**: 

When you measure a quantum system, it "collapses" from a superposition of states into one of its possible states with a probability dictated by the squared magnitude of the coefficients in the superposition. This is known as the Born rule.

3. **Uncertainty Principle: 

Quantum states are subject to the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle, which states that certain pairs of properties (like position and momentum) cannot be precisely known simultaneously. This introduces inherent uncertainty into quantum measurements.

**Quantum Entanglement:

Quantum entanglement is a phenomenon that occurs when two or more quantum particles become correlated in such a way that the properties of one particle are dependent on the properties of another, even when they are separated by large distances. The key features of entanglement are:

1. **Entangled Pairs**: 

Entanglement is often observed in pairs of particles, such as photons. These pairs are created in a way that their properties become interdependent.

2. **Non-Local Correlations**: 

When two entangled particles are measured, the results are correlated instantaneously, even if they are far apart. This correlation doesn't obey classical notions of cause and effect.

3. **No Information Transfer**: 

Importantly, entanglement doesn't allow for faster-than-light communication. While measurements on entangled particles are correlated, they cannot be used to transmit information at speeds greater than the speed of light.

4. **Bell's Theorem**: 

Bell's theorem is a fundamental result in quantum physics that provides a way to test for the presence of entanglement. Experiments based on Bell's theorem have consistently shown that quantum entanglement is a real phenomenon.
